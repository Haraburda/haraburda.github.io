These bars (or columns, if you’re feeling fancy) can stand tall or lay flat depending on how you set them up. In Figure 1, the bars are standing upright, loud and proud, and it’s pretty clear that item B is the overachiever of the group.

Bullet Graph

This is like the speedometer on your dashboard; it shows one number on a set scale and looks pretty cool doing it. But when it comes to actually understanding data, the bullet graph is the smarter, more practical cousin. It lays everything out in a clean, straight line so your eyes don’t have to work overtime. In Figure 3, you can see the monthly averages, with item B chilling comfortably in the middle range (20–29), right where the graph says, “Yep, that seems about right”.

Marimekko Graph

This chart, called a mosaic graph, is basically a stacked chart that decided to go 2‑D and show off. Instead of just stacking things up, it also stretches the columns wider or narrower to show how different categories relate to each other. In Figure 6, item B is clearly the star of the 20–29 range, taking up more space than the other two items and making sure everyone notices.

Sankey

This is like an air‑traffic controller of charts as it shows you exactly where everything is flowing and how much of it is moving around. The thicker the connecting lines (called links), the bigger the flow. It’s perfect for tracking how resources like hours, materials, or general chaos move through different stages, known as nodes. In Figure 7, you can follow the journey as each month sends its flow into items A and B, which then funnel into item C, before everything branches right back out into each month again. It’s basically a very organized data round‑trip.

This is a waffle graph, which is what you get when data decides to express itself through tiny, evenly sized shapes. Think of it as a pixel‑art version of your dataset. In the 10×10 waffle setup shown in Figure 8, a solid 53% of the items land in the 10–19 range, which is noticeably higher than the counts in the other two ranges. Basically, that range is doing all the heavy lifting while the others are just along for the ride.

In Figure 9, you can see items A and B adding to the pile, item C subtracting from it, and the whole thing settling at a neat net total of 307 counts. It’s like watching your data balance its checkbook.
